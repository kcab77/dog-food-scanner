Medicinal Mushrooms for Dogs: The Evidence, the Best Types, and What Actually Works

Medicinal mushrooms have been used in human medicine for thousands of years. In veterinary medicine, they're one of the fastest-growing areas of integrative care — and unlike most trends in pet health, the research behind several of these mushrooms is genuinely compelling. A University of Pennsylvania clinical trial. Published studies on immune modulation, cancer, insulin resistance, and liver function. Real data, not just anecdote.

Here's what the evidence actually says about the most studied mushrooms for dogs.

How Medicinal Mushrooms Work

The primary active compounds in medicinal mushrooms are beta-glucans — complex polysaccharides found in the cell walls of the mushroom. Beta-glucans are biological response modifiers: they don't directly attack pathogens or tumors, but they modulate the immune system's ability to recognize and respond to them. [Study]

Different mushroom species contain different beta-glucan profiles, and many also contain unique secondary compounds — triterpenes, ergosterol (a precursor to Vitamin D), cordycepin, and others — that have their own documented biological effects.

One critical note on sourcing: the active compounds are concentrated in the fruiting body (the actual mushroom) not the mycelium (the root structure). Many cheap supplements are mycelium grown on grain — which is mostly starch with very low beta-glucan content. Always look for products specifying "fruiting body" and listing beta-glucan percentage. Turkey Tail (Trametes versicolor)

What the Evidence Shows

Turkey tail has the strongest clinical evidence of any medicinal mushroom in dogs. A randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trial at the University of Pennsylvania School of Veterinary Medicine gave turkey tail extract to dogs with hemangiosarcoma — one of the most aggressive cancers in dogs. Dogs receiving the highest dose had the longest median survival times ever reported for this disease without chemotherapy. [University of Pennsylvania Study]

The active compounds — PSK (polysaccharide-K) and PSP (polysaccharide-peptide) — are among the most studied anti-cancer compounds in both human and veterinary oncology. PSK is an approved cancer treatment drug in Japan. [Study on PSK/PSP]

Turkey tail also supports gut microbiome diversity. A human clinical trial found it significantly increased populations of beneficial Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus while reducing pathogenic bacteria — relevant for dogs with chronic digestive issues. [Study]

Best Uses for Dogs

  • Dogs with cancer (as complementary therapy alongside conventional treatment)
  • Dogs with compromised immune function
  • Dogs with chronic digestive issues or poor microbiome diversity
  • Senior dogs for general immune support

Dose

The University of Pennsylvania trial used 25–100 mg/kg per day. Most commercial products for dogs recommend starting at the lower end. Always use a product specifying fruiting body and beta-glucan content.

Reishi (Ganoderma lucidum)

What the Evidence Shows

Reishi is often called the "mushroom of immortality" in traditional Chinese medicine — and while that's marketing language, the research on its specific mechanisms is substantial.

Reishi activates AMPK (AMP-activated protein kinase) — the same cellular energy sensor activated by exercise and caloric restriction. AMPK activation reduces insulin resistance, improves fat metabolism, and inhibits the inflammatory pathways that drive chronic disease. [Study]

Separately, reishi's triterpenes directly inhibit adipocyte differentiation — the process by which stem cells become fat cells. This is directly relevant to lipoma management. [Study]

Reishi also has documented hepatoprotective effects — protecting liver cells from oxidative damage and supporting liver detoxification pathways. For dogs on long-term medications including monthly heartworm preventatives, this is a meaningful benefit. [Study]

Its beta-glucans support immune modulation, and its anti-inflammatory triterpenes inhibit histamine release — potentially relevant for dogs with allergies. [Study]

Best Uses for Dogs

  • Dogs with lipomas or tendency toward fat deposits
  • Dogs on long-term medications (liver support)
  • Dogs with allergies or chronic inflammation
  • Overweight or insulin-resistant dogs
  • Senior dogs for general longevity support

Dose

Typical veterinary recommendations: 25–50 mg/kg per day of a standardized extract. Reishi has a bitter taste — mixing with food or using a capsule helps with palatability.

Maitake (Grifola frondosa)

What the Evidence Shows

Maitake's primary documented mechanism is improving insulin sensitivity and lowering blood glucose. A study in insulin-resistant mice found maitake extract significantly reduced circulating glucose and insulin levels. [Study] This is directly relevant for overweight dogs and dogs with lipomas, since high insulin drives fat storage in peripheral tissue.

Maitake also contains the D-fraction — a specific beta-glucan extract shown to activate macrophages and natural killer cells, enhancing immune surveillance against abnormal cells. Multiple studies have examined D-fraction's anti-tumor activity in rodent models and human clinical settings. [Study]

Best Uses for Dogs

  • Overweight or diabetic dogs
  • Dogs with lipomas (insulin-driven fat storage)
  • Dogs with cancer (complementary immune support)
  • Dogs with metabolic syndrome

Dose

Most veterinary integrative practitioners recommend 1–3 mg/kg per day of standardized maitake D-fraction, or follow product label for whole fruiting body powder.

Chaga (Inonotus obliquus)

What the Evidence Shows

Chaga is one of the highest antioxidant-containing foods measured by ORAC score — higher than blueberries, acai, or most other commonly cited antioxidant sources. Its primary active compounds include betulinic acid, inotodiol, and melanin pigments with strong free radical scavenging activity. [Study]

Chaga has demonstrated anti-inflammatory effects through inhibition of NF-kB — the same inflammatory pathway targeted by curcumin and many pharmaceutical anti-inflammatories. [Study]

Betulinic acid, derived from birch bark (Chaga grows on birch trees and concentrates this compound), has shown anti-tumor activity in multiple cancer cell line studies. [Study]

Important caution: Chaga is high in oxalates. Dogs prone to calcium oxalate bladder or kidney stones (Miniature Schnauzers, Bichon Frise, Yorkshire Terriers, Lhasa Apsos) should avoid it or use with vet guidance.

Best Uses for Dogs

  • Dogs with chronic inflammation or inflammatory conditions
  • Dogs with cancer (complementary antioxidant support)
  • Dogs exposed to high oxidative stress (outdoor, active, older dogs)
  • Not recommended for dogs prone to oxalate stones

Lion's Mane (Hericium erinaceus)

What the Evidence Shows

Lion's Mane is unique among medicinal mushrooms for its documented effects on the nervous system. Its active compounds — hericenones and erinacines — stimulate production of Nerve Growth Factor (NGF), a protein essential for the growth and maintenance of neurons. [Study]

A double-blind, placebo-controlled human clinical trial found significant improvement in mild cognitive impairment with lion's mane supplementation over 16 weeks. [Study] While direct canine trials are limited, the neurological mechanisms are well-established and several veterinary neurologists are using it as adjunctive support for dogs with degenerative myelopathy, cognitive dysfunction syndrome, and post-injury nerve recovery.

Lion's mane also has documented gastrointestinal benefits — protecting the stomach lining and supporting recovery from gastric ulcers. [Study]

Best Uses for Dogs

  • Senior dogs with cognitive dysfunction (dog dementia)
  • Dogs with degenerative myelopathy or nerve damage
  • Dogs recovering from neurological injury or surgery
  • Dogs with chronic gastrointestinal issues

Cordyceps (Cordyceps militaris)

What the Evidence Shows

Cordyceps has the most documented effects on energy metabolism and athletic performance of any medicinal mushroom. Its primary compound, cordycepin, activates AMPK, improving cellular energy production and oxygen utilization. [Study]

Cordyceps also has documented kidney-protective effects — clinical trials in humans with chronic kidney disease showed it slowed progression and reduced creatinine levels. [Study] Given that kidney disease is one of the leading causes of death in senior dogs, this is a meaningful area of interest.

Note on sourcing: Look for Cordyceps militaris (farmed) rather than wild C. sinensis — it contains higher cordycepin content and is more consistently available without adulteration. [Study]

Best Uses for Dogs

  • Active or working dogs for endurance and recovery
  • Dogs with early kidney disease
  • Senior dogs with low energy or chronic fatigue
  • Dogs undergoing chemotherapy (fatigue support)

Shiitake (Lentinula edodes)

What the Evidence Shows

Shiitake contains lentinan — a beta-1,3/1,6-glucan that is one of the most studied immunomodulatory compounds in oncology. Lentinan is an approved biological response modifier used in cancer treatment in Japan. [Study]

Shiitake also contains eritadenine, shown to lower LDL cholesterol and improve fat metabolism. [Study] And it's one of the few non-animal food sources of complete protein, making it a genuinely nutritious whole food addition to a dog's diet.

Always cook shiitake before feeding — raw shiitake can cause a skin rash reaction (shiitake dermatitis) in some dogs.

Best Uses for Dogs

  • Dogs with cancer (complementary immune support)
  • Dogs with high cholesterol or fat metabolism issues
  • Can be fed as cooked whole food — chop and add to meals

How to Choose a Quality Mushroom Supplement

The mushroom supplement market has a serious quality problem. Many products are made from mycelium on grain — mostly starch, low active compounds — and are not tested for beta-glucan content.

What to look for:

  • Fruiting body — specified on the label
  • Beta-glucan percentage — listed (typically 20–40% for quality products)
  • Hot water extracted — beta-glucans require hot water extraction to be bioavailable
  • Third-party tested — certificate of analysis available
  • Low starch content — if starch is high, it's mostly mycelium filler
