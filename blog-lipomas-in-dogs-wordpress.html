Lipomas in Dogs: What They Are, Which Breeds Are Most at Risk, and What You Can Do About Them

If you've ever felt a soft, squishy lump under your dog's skin and panicked, you're not alone. Lipomas — benign fatty tumors — are one of the most common things vets find on middle-aged and senior dogs. Most are harmless. But "harmless" doesn't mean you should ignore them, and there's growing evidence that diet and specific supplements can slow their development or reduce their size.

Here's what you actually need to know.

What Is a Lipoma?

A lipoma is a benign (non-cancerous) mass made up of fat cells that grows just beneath the skin. They're typically soft, movable, and painless. They grow slowly and in most cases don't affect your dog's quality of life.

The problem is when they grow large enough to restrict movement — especially in the armpit, groin, or between the legs — or when they grow deeper into muscle tissue. These are called infiltrative lipomas and they're a different situation. They don't have clean borders, they can't be easily removed, and they can come back after surgery.

Any new lump should always be checked by a vet. A fine needle aspirate — a simple, low-cost in-office procedure — can tell you within minutes whether you're dealing with a lipoma or something that needs more attention.

Which Breeds Are Most Prone to Lipomas?

A large UK study of over 384,000 dogs found lipomas are most common in dogs aged 9–12, dogs above their ideal body weight, and certain breeds. Spayed females had the highest odds of any group.

The breeds most commonly affected include:

  • Labrador Retrievers — one of the highest-risk breeds, especially as they age
  • Weimaraners — among the highest breed risk in research studies
  • Doberman Pinschers — prone to both standard and infiltrative lipomas
  • Golden Retrievers — very commonly affected
  • Miniature Schnauzers — high fat metabolism predisposes them
  • Cocker Spaniels
  • Mixed breeds — also commonly affected, especially overweight dogs

Overweight dogs of any breed have roughly twice the risk of developing lipomas compared to dogs at a healthy weight. Age is also a major factor — risk increases dramatically after age 8.

What Causes Lipomas?

Veterinary science doesn't have one single definitive cause. But research consistently points to these drivers:

  • Chronic inflammation — lipoma tissue contains elevated numbers of actively proliferating fat stem cells driven by inflammatory signals. Reducing systemic inflammation is one of the most direct ways to slow lipoma formation. [Study]
  • Insulin resistance and excess carbohydrates — when cells become insulin resistant, the body stores more fat in peripheral tissue. High-starch kibble diets drive this cycle. [Study]
  • Poor omega-6 to omega-3 ratio — most commercial kibble has an omega-6 to omega-3 ratio of 10:1 to 20:1. The ideal is closer to 5:1 or lower. An imbalanced ratio creates a pro-inflammatory environment that drives abnormal fat cell growth.
  • Impaired liver function — the liver is the central organ of fat metabolism. When it can't process lipids efficiently, excess fat gets pushed into peripheral storage under the skin. [Study]
  • Poor lymphatic drainage — the lymphatic system relies entirely on movement to circulate. Impaired lymph flow is directly linked to reduced fat breakdown in adipose tissue. [Study]
  • Genetics — predisposed breeds carry inherited tendencies regardless of diet

Diet Changes That Help

Diet is the most powerful lever available. The goal is to reduce insulin spikes, lower systemic inflammation, and support liver function.

  • Cut ultra-processed carbohydrates — kibble high in starch drives insulin resistance and fat storage. Switching to fresh, whole-food, or raw food reduces the metabolic burden on the liver and lowers circulating insulin
  • Fix the omega ratio — add whole food omega-3 sources (sardines, salmon, mackerel) several times per week. Whole food fish is better absorbed than capsules
  • Remove inflammatory oils — sunflower, safflower, corn, soybean, and vegetable oil are all high omega-6 and pro-inflammatory. Check your dog's ingredient label
  • Avoid peas, lentils, and soy — pro-inflammatory and associated with additional metabolic concerns
  • Lose the extra weight — even modest weight loss reduces the inflammatory signals that drive fat cell proliferation

Supplements That May Help Prevent or Slow Lipoma Growth

1. Fish Oil (Omega-3 / EPA + DHA)

The most important supplement for lipoma-prone dogs. Research in dogs specifically shows fish oil lowers blood glucose, raises adiponectin (an anti-inflammatory fat hormone), and improves how the body handles stored fat. [Study] [Study]

Dose: approximately 20–55 mg EPA/DHA per kg of body weight per day. Consult your vet for your dog's specific needs.

2. Turmeric / Curcumin

Curcumin directly inhibits fat cell formation and suppresses the blood vessel growth that allows fat deposits to expand. [Study] It also reduces the macrophage-driven inflammation in fat tissue that triggers new lipoma growth. [Study]

Bioavailability is the key — curcumin is poorly absorbed without black pepper (piperine), which increases absorption by up to 2,000%. The classic preparation is "golden paste" — turmeric powder, coconut oil, and black pepper mixed into food.

General guideline: 15–20 mg per kg of body weight daily. Always start small and increase gradually.

3. Milk Thistle (Liver Support)

Silymarin, the active compound in milk thistle, is one of the most well-studied hepatoprotective herbs. A healthy liver that efficiently processes fats is less likely to push excess lipids into peripheral fat storage. Especially valuable for dogs on long-term medications like monthly heartworm preventatives.

4. Digestive Enzymes

Some holistic veterinarians recommend systemic enzyme therapy for lipoma management — specifically enzymes like lipase, bromelain, and papain that may help the body break down and reabsorb fatty tissue. Adding a digestive enzyme supplement to every meal supports fat metabolism while also improving overall nutrient absorption.

5. Medicinal Mushrooms

Several mushrooms directly address the metabolic root causes of lipomas:

  • Reishi — reduces insulin resistance via AMPK activation (the same cellular pathway activated by exercise) and directly inhibits fat cell differentiation. [Study] [Study]
  • Maitake — enhances insulin sensitivity and lowers circulating glucose and insulin in insulin-resistant models. [Study]
  • Turkey Tail — supports immune surveillance, helping the body respond to abnormal tissue. A University of Pennsylvania study found it extended survival times in dogs with cancer.
  • Chaga — reduces the systemic inflammation that creates the environment for lipoma development

6. Exercise and Lymphatic Drainage

The lymphatic system has no pump — it relies entirely on movement. Research shows that impaired lymphatic drainage directly reduces the body's ability to break down fat in adipose tissue. [Study] Daily exercise is not optional for lipoma-prone dogs. Even 30 minutes of walking stimulates lymphatic flow, improves insulin sensitivity, and reduces inflammatory cytokines. For dogs with mobility issues, gentle massage along the lymph nodes (neck, armpits, groin) provides similar benefit.

When to Have a Lipoma Removed

Most vets recommend monitoring small, stable lipomas rather than removing them. Surgery carries anesthesia risk — especially in older dogs — and lipomas often grow back. The exceptions:

  • The lipoma is restricting movement or causing pain
  • It's growing rapidly (more than 20% in a few months)
  • Location will likely cause problems — armpit, between the legs, near a joint
  • Fine needle aspirate shows anything other than pure fat cells

If your vet recommends monitoring, photograph and measure the lipoma monthly so you have an objective record of growth over time.
