# 🔬 Synthetic vitamins & minerals — which forms actually matter

**Reading a kibble label, bottom half.** The mineral and vitamin block is where the cheap foods and
the good ones actually separate — and it's the part almost nobody can read.

---

## The one rule that covers most of it

**Same mineral, three grades of form. The word after the metal is what you're reading.**

```
CHELATED  ▸ proteinate · amino acid chelate · amino acid complex · methionine · "yeast"
            → bound to protein, absorbed like food.  BEST

SULFATE   ▸ zinc sulfate · ferrous sulfate · manganese sulfate
            → inorganic, absorbed adequately, pro-oxidant.  MIDDLE

OXIDE     ▸ zinc oxide · manganese oxide · iron oxide · copper oxide
            → barely absorbed. Cheapest to buy, closest to useless.  WORST
```

**This isn't holistic opinion. It's regulatory:** AAFCO **forbids copper oxide from counting toward a
food's copper minimum at all**, citing *"very poor apparent digestibility."* A regulator saying an
ingredient can't count as the nutrient it's named after is as clear as this gets.

**On a label:** `Zinc Proteinate` beats `Zinc Sulfate` beats `Zinc Oxide`. Every time. Same money to
the dog, very different money to the manufacturer.

---

## ⚠️ Copper is the exception — and it runs backwards

**For every other mineral, better absorbed = better. For copper, better absorbed can be the problem.**

Copper accumulates in the liver and dogs have no good way to dump the excess. Since AAFCO **deleted the
copper maximum in 2007** — and it still doesn't exist — foods moved to highly bioavailable copper forms
with no ceiling on the total.

So on a copper-predisposed dog:

| Breed group | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Bedlington Terrier · West Highland White · Doberman · Labrador · Dalmatian** | Documented copper-storage predisposition. Labradors are the one most owners don't know about. |

- **Copper-restricted target: under ~1.2 mg per 1,000 kcal** *(Today's Veterinary Practice)*
- **Liver enzymes are NOT sensitive** for the early stages — normal bloodwork doesn't rule it out
- **There is no AAFCO maximum**, so "meets AAFCO" tells you nothing about the copper ceiling

**Don't apply "chelated is always better" blindly to copper.** For a Lab, total copper load matters more
than form.

## The minerals, exactly

| Mineral | ✅ Look for | ⚠️ Acceptable | ❌ Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Zinc** | zinc proteinate · zinc amino acid chelate · zinc methionine | zinc sulfate | **zinc oxide** |
| **Iron** | iron proteinate · iron amino acid chelate | ferrous sulfate | **iron oxide** |
| **Manganese** | manganese proteinate · manganese amino acid chelate | manganese sulfate | **manganese oxide** |
| **Copper** | copper proteinate · copper amino acid chelate — **but watch total load** | copper sulfate | **copper oxide** *(can't legally count toward the minimum)* |
| **Selenium** | **selenium yeast / selenomethionine** | — | **sodium selenite · sodium selenate** |
| **Magnesium** | magnesium proteinate | magnesium sulfate | **magnesium oxide** |

**Selenium is the one with the narrowest margin.** AAFCO sets **0.35 mg/kg minimum and 2 mg/kg maximum**
— that's a maximum only about 6× the minimum, tighter than almost any other nutrient. Organic selenium
(yeast) is both better absorbed and better tolerated than the inorganic salts.

## Vitamins — and the honest part

**Most synthetic vitamins are fine.** The panic about "40 added vitamins" is mostly misplaced, and
overstating it is how you lose credibility with anyone who knows nutrition.

| Vitamin | What to know |
|---|---|
| **Vitamin E** | **`d-alpha-tocopherol` (natural) beats `dl-alpha-tocopherol` (synthetic).** The `dl-` is a 50/50 mix of mirror-image molecules and the dog can only use half of it. **One letter, half the vitamin.** |
| **Vitamin K3 — menadione** | The genuinely contested one. Menadione sodium bisulfite complex. **FDA banned it from human OTC supplements; still permitted in animal feed.** Depletes glutathione and generates reactive oxygen species — *mechanism only.* Over 50+ years there is **no published report of nutritional toxicity in dogs at pet-food levels.** Prefer foods without it; don't claim it's proven to harm dogs. |
| **Vitamin A** | Retinyl acetate / palmitate. Fine. **Dogs tolerate vitamin A far better than most species** — AAFCO's ceiling is 250,000 IU/kg against a 5,000 minimum. Not the thing to worry about. |
| **Vitamin D** | The one with a real narrow range: **500 IU/kg minimum, 3,000 maximum.** Recalls have happened from over-fortification. Cholecalciferol (D3) is the right form. |
| **Thiamine** | Mononitrate or hydrochloride — both fine. Thiamine is heat-fragile, which is *why* it's added back. |
| **Ascorbic acid (C)** | **Dogs synthesise their own vitamin C.** Its presence is neither good nor bad — it's often there as a preservative, not a nutrient. |
| **Choline chloride** | Fine and genuinely needed. Ignore it. |

## What a long vitamin list actually tells you

**Not "these vitamins are dangerous."** The honest read:

> **A 40-item synthetic premix means the ingredients before it didn't deliver the nutrients.**
> Extrusion at high heat destroys them, so they're sprayed back on afterwards. The list is a
> *symptom of the processing*, not a poison in itself.

**And the reverse is also true, which trips people up:** a food with *no* added vitamins and minerals
isn't automatically superior — it may simply be incomplete. A fresh food that hits requirements from
actual ingredients is the goal; a fresh food that misses them is worse than fortified kibble.

**Ask "why did this food need 40 additions?" — not "are these 40 additions toxic?"**

## Reading the label in order — 60 seconds

1. **First five ingredients.** Named meat first? Or a grain/legume in the top three?
2. **Named vs generic.** `Chicken meal` ✅ · `Poultry meal` / `Meat meal` / `Animal digest` ❌ — generic means the species isn't disclosed, which means it varies batch to batch.
3. **Fat source and its preservative.** `Chicken fat preserved with mixed tocopherols` ✅ · `preserved with BHA/BHT` ❌ · `Animal fat` ❌ (unnamed species again).
4. **Jump to the mineral block.** Count the **oxides**. Two or more = the manufacturer bought the cheapest forms available, and that tells you what they did everywhere else you can't see.
5. **Check selenium's form.** Yeast ✅ · selenite/selenate ❌.
6. **Check vitamin E.** `d-` ✅ · `dl-` ⚠️.
7. **Scan for menadione.** Present = prefer another bag; not a reason to panic.

**Step 4 is the highest-signal move on the whole label.** Mineral forms are invisible to marketing —
no bag advertises "we use proteinates" on the front — so they're one of the few honest tells left.

## 🔧 Gaps in the app's own scoring (found 2026-08-20)

The scanner currently misses several of these. Worth fixing:

| Term | Status | Suggested |
|---|---|---|
| `ferrous sulfate` | **not scored** | mild |
| `manganese oxide` | **not scored** | mild–moderate |
| `iron oxide` | **not scored** | mild–moderate |
| `dl-alpha-tocopherol` | **not scored** | mild |
| `copper oxide` | **check** | moderate — AAFCO won't let it count toward the minimum |
| `copper sulfate` | currently **severe** | ⚠️ inconsistent — see below |
| `zinc oxide` | currently **mild** | fine, but then copper sulfate at `severe` doesn't line up |

**The inconsistency to resolve:** `copper sulfate` sits at **severe** while `zinc oxide` sits at **mild**.
Under the evidence-tier rule (severity capped by what's actually demonstrated in dogs), copper sulfate
being flagged is defensible — **but for the opposite reason to every other entry.** It's flagged for
being *too* absorbable in a species with no copper ceiling, not for being a poor form. **If that reason
isn't written into the entry text, the tier looks arbitrary to anyone who checks.**
