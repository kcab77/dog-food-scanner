# 🧪 Synthetic vs natural — where the difference is real, and where it isn't

**The anxiety this addresses:** *"food should deliver the nutrients, and synthetic can't be as good."*

**Half of that is right. The half that's wrong is the half causing the worry.**

---

## Where "synthetic ≠ natural" is FALSE

For most vitamins, **the synthetic molecule is chemically identical to the food one.** Not similar —
identical. There is no test that can tell them apart in the dog, because there is nothing to tell apart.

- **Ascorbic acid** is ascorbic acid
- **Thiamine** is thiamine
- **Niacin, riboflavin, B6, B12, folate, biotin, pantothenic acid** — same molecule either way
- **Choline chloride** — fine, and genuinely needed

**Seeing these on a label is not a warning sign.** Reacting to them costs credibility with anyone who
knows nutrition, and it spends worry you could spend somewhere it matters.

---

## Where the difference is REAL — the short list

**Four. That's the whole list.**

| Nutrient | The real difference |
|---|---|
| **Vitamin E** | `d-alpha-tocopherol` (natural) vs `dl-alpha-tocopherol` (synthetic). The `dl-` is a 50/50 mix of mirror-image molecules and **the dog can only use one of them.** One letter, roughly half the vitamin. |
| **Selenium** | `selenium yeast` / selenomethionine (organic) vs `sodium selenite` / `selenate`. Different absorption, different tolerance — and selenium has the **narrowest safety margin on the whole panel** (AAFCO max is only ~6× the minimum). |
| **Minerals generally** | `proteinate` / `amino acid chelate` vs `sulfate` vs `oxide`. AAFCO **won't let copper oxide count toward a food's copper minimum** — *"very poor apparent digestibility."* |
| **Vitamin K** | `Menadione` (K3) is synthetic with no natural equivalent in food. The genuinely contested one. |

**That's it.** Four places where the form on the label changes what the dog gets. **Everything else is
the same molecule.**

---

## ⚠️ The list that actually matters: where dogs are POOR converters

**This is the real answer to "shouldn't food deliver everything?" — because for these, food often
can't, and the gap is species-specific.**

| Conversion | How well dogs do it | What it means in the bowl |
|---|---|---|
| **ALA → EPA/DHA** *(flax, chia → marine omega-3)* | **Poorly.** Single digits to low double digits. | **Flaxseed does not replace fish oil.** A food listing flax as its omega-3 source has not given your dog meaningful EPA/DHA. This is the biggest practical gap on the list. |
| **Beta-carotene → vitamin A** *(carrots → retinol)* | **Inefficiently.** Dogs can; cats can't at all. | Carrots are not a reliable vitamin A source. Liver and animal fats are. |
| **Sun → vitamin D** | **Essentially not at all.** Dogs synthesise almost no vitamin D in skin, unlike humans. | **It must come from the diet, every day.** No amount of sunbathing helps. This is the one people are most surprised by. |
| **Cysteine → taurine** | **Yes** — dogs can synthesise taurine (cats can't). | But large breeds on low-meat diets can still fall short. **Relevant to a Labrador.** |
| **Tryptophan → niacin** | **Yes.** | Not a gap worth tracking. |
| **Gut bacteria → vitamin K** | **Partly.** | Contributes, doesn't cover everything. |

**Read the pattern:** dogs are poor converters in exactly the places where *"just feed whole food"*
quietly fails. **That's not an argument against fresh feeding — it's the list of what a fresh diet has
to deliver directly rather than assume.**

## The answer to the fear about no-added-vitamins food

**The question was never "added or not added." It's "does the food hit requirements from its
ingredients?"**

**And for Hershey's food, we already checked — on 2026-08-14, against the AAFCO PDF:**

> **AllProvide beef and chicken pass every AAFCO minimum AND every maximum.** Their published
> dry-matter figures match independent calculation to one decimal place.

**That is not luck and it is not marketing.** It's a formulated diet built to hit the numbers from
actual ingredients, and it was verified against the regulatory document, not a blog.

**So the specific worry — "no added vitamins scares me" — is the one worry you can put down.**
That food was checked. It passed.

**Where the worry stays useful:** if you ever move to a home-cooked diet, that guarantee disappears
with it. **95% of home-prepared recipes tested short on at least one nutrient** (Stockman 2013, 200
recipes) — most often **zinc and copper.** The formulation is doing invisible work.
